Alright, let's yap about Model Predictive Control (MPC) because, honestly, calling it just "a controller" is doing it dirty. It’s not some rigid, out-of-the-box algorithm like a standard PID loop that you tune once and forget.

MPC is an entire philosophy and family of control strategies.

At its core, the classic MPC is a control framework that uses a system model to predict future behavior, solves an online optimization problem over a finite horizon, and executes only the first step before doing it all over again. But the "model" and the "optimization" parts of that sentence are where things get wild. Right now, the bleeding edge of research isn't focused on standard linear MPC; it's pushing deep into Stochastic MPC and Neural/AI-driven MPC.

1. Stochastic MPC (Dealing with a Chaotic World)
Classic MPC is deterministic. It assumes your model is perfect and the environment is predictable. If you tell it there's a 10% chance of a massive gust of wind, it usually just shrugs or handles it via rigid, over-conservative "robust" margins that kill performance.

By using stochastic analysis, probability density functions, and martingale theory, researchers are designing MPCs that can dynamically calculate risks. This is huge for space exploration, drone flight in turbulent weather, and smart power grids dealing with the volatile nature of renewable energy.

2. Full-Blown Neural Network MPC (Deep MPC)
If you want to talk about what's dominating academic papers right now, it's the marriage of MPC and Deep Learning. Traditionally, physics-based models (like differential equations) are used in MPC. But what if the system is too complex to model with physics? Like a soft robot, a chemical refinery, or an aerodynamic hull at hypersonic speeds?

Enter the Neural MPC ecosystem, which generally splits into three flavors:

A. The Neural Network as the Model
Instead of deriving equations, you train a Deep Neural Network (like an LSTM or a Neural ODE) on historical data to learn the system's dynamics. The MPC then uses this neural net to predict the future states during its optimization loop.

B. The Neural Network as the Optimization Solver
Solving a massive non-linear optimization problem in real-time (every few milliseconds) is computationally brutal. Researchers are training neural networks to imitate the MPC solver. The network learns the mapping from "current state" to "optimal control action." Once trained, you can run this "Explicit MPC" on cheap hardware because running a neural network forward pass is incredibly fast compared to solving an active-set optimization problem.

C. Reinforcement Learning (RL) + MPC Hybrids
Pure RL is great at learning complex behaviors but has zero safety guarantees—it learns by crashing and burning. MPC is safe but computationally heavy and rigid. The current holy grail of research is using MPC as a safety filter for RL, or using RL to learn the cost functions and terminal ingredients for the MPC.

The Big Research Bottleneck
Why isn't this everywhere yet? Because control engineers are obsessed with two things: Stability and Feasibility.

If you put a standard deep neural network inside a closed-loop control system, it is incredibly difficult to mathematically prove that the system won't randomly explode or become unstable if it encounters an out-of-distribution data point. Researchers are currently sweating over things like Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) and Lyapunov stability constraints embedded directly into network layers to force these AI-driven MPCs to be provably safe.

So yeah, MPC isn't a single tool; it's a massive, evolving playground where statistics, optimization, and deep learning are colliding to figure out how machines can safely navigate an unpredictable future.

A significant portion of academic research focuses on "pure data-driven control" or "deep neural network controllers" because it makes for high-impact papers. Putting a full-size PC or an expensive edge-computing GPU inside a compact, cost-sensitive mechanical system (like a consumer appliance, an automotive sub-assembly, or a small drone) is a commercial and engineering dead end.

1. The Core Arguments: Why the Plant Model Matters
When arguing for a model-based workflow over pure data-driven control on resource-constrained hardware, your defense rests on three pillars: Computational Parsimony, Provable Stability, and The Separation Principle.

A. Computational Parsimony (The "MCU Slaving" Problem)
A micro-controller unit (MCU) running at 80–200 MHz with limited RAM cannot afford to compute a massive non-linear optimization loop or run a deep neural network forward-pass while simultaneously handling safety interrupts, CAN-bus telemetry, and sensor filtering (Salzmann et al., 2022).

By finding the actual plant model (even a simplified linear approximation around an operating point), you can compute the control law offline.

Example: In Model Predictive Control, if you have a linear plant model, you can pre-compute the entire optimization problem using parametric programming. This turns a heavy online optimization solver into a simple Explicit MPC lookup table (a series of piecewise linear functions) that an ultra-cheap Cortex-M MCU can execute in microseconds.

B. The Guarantee of Stability (Lyapunov Criteria)
Pure data-driven or black-box neural network controllers cannot easily prove closed-loop stability (Tang & Daoutidis, 2022). If an asset encounters an out-of-distribution state (a physical shock or a sudden load change), a neural network can behave unpredictably.

Having an explicit plant model allows you to use classic control theory (Lyapunov stability, frequency domain margins, or Passivity theorems) to mathematically guarantee that the system will never explode or enter destructive oscillations.

2. Key Methodologies to Search For
To find papers proving that finding/interpolating a model is superior for cheap implementation, stop looking for "AI control" and use these specific control-theory terms:

A. Indirect Data-Driven Control (System Identification)
Academic literature differentiates between Direct data-driven control (going straight from data to controller, skipping the model) and Indirect data-driven control (using data to find the plant model first, then designing the controller).

The Argument: Indirect methods are highly efficient. You collect data from the physical system, use algorithms like Subspace System Identification (SID) or Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD) to build a compact state-space model, and then design a lightweight PID, LQR, or classical lead-lag compensator. The data is used to find the physics, not to act as the live controller.

B. Surrogate Modeling & Model Order Reduction (MOR)
If the plant dynamics are incredibly complex (e.g., fluid dynamics, thermal fields, or complex mechanical linkages), you cannot run the full physics equations on an MCU.

Look up Model Order Reduction (MOR). This is a massive field of engineering dedicated to taking a high-fidelity, computationally unfeasible system model and mathematically shrinking it down to a low-order differential equation that retains 95%+ of the original performance dynamics but can run effortlessly on an MCU (PMC12390536).

C. Gain Scheduling & Data Interpolation
If your system is highly non-linear, you don't need a deep neural network to handle it. The classic, cheaper industry alternative is Gain Scheduling via Data Interpolation.

You linearize the plant model at several distinct operating points (e.g., a drone hovering at 0 m/s, flying at 5 m/s, and flying at 10 m/s). You design a cheap, linear controller (like an LQR or PID) for each point. On the MCU, you simply interpolate between these controller gains based on the live data (speed). This achieves non-linear tracking performance using negligible processing power.

For example, recent literature demonstrates building digital twins and thermal/mechanical estimators directly on STM32 microcontrollers by relying strictly on first-principles/Laplace equations rather than heavy black-box ML models (PMC12390536). They explicitly prove that anchoring the software to a known physical plant model drastically reduces latency and resource consumption.
